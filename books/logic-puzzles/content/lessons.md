# Lesson 1

Three neighbors, Hattie, Gus and Wren, each brought one pie to the spring bake sale at the church hall: apple, cherry or peach. Nobody brought two, and no two brought the same kind. Here are the clues.

[[clues L1]]

## The grid

The names run down the side. The pies run across the top. Each square asks one question: does this person go with this pie? Right now every square is blank, and blank means undecided.

[[grid L1 | caption: Nothing is decided yet.]]

## Two marks

- An **X** [X] means no.
- A **dot** [O] means yes.

## Clue 1: a yes

"Gus brought the cherry pie." Put a dot where Gus's row meets the cherry column.

Now the useful part. Gus brought only one pie, so the other squares in his row are X's. And only one person brought the cherry pie, so the other squares in the cherry column are X's too.

> **One dot brings its X's with it.** Every time you place a dot, cross out the rest of its row and the rest of its column.

[[grid L1 | O: Gus=cherry | new: Gus=cherry | caption: Clue 1 gives one dot and four X's.]]

## Clue 2: a no

"Wren did not bring the apple pie." That is an X where Wren meets apple.

## The only choice left

Look at Wren's row. Apple has an X from clue 2. Cherry has an X from clue 1. Only peach is left, so Wren brought the peach pie. Then Hattie's row has one blank square left: apple.

[[grid L1 | O: Gus=cherry | X: Wren=apple | new: Wren=apple | caption: Clue 2 adds an X. || O: Gus=cherry, Wren=peach, Hattie=apple | new: Wren=peach, Hattie=apple | caption: One choice left in each row.]]

So Hattie brought apple, Gus brought cherry, and Wren brought peach. Every clue is true, and every row and column has exactly one dot. That is how you know you are done.

## Other clue words

- "Wren brought **either** the apple **or** the peach pie." Wren goes with one of those two. Put an X on every other pie in Wren's row.
- "Wren brought **neither** the apple **nor** the peach pie." Two X's, one for each.
- "The apple pie's baker is not Hattie." The same as "Hattie did not bring the apple pie." Clues can name a person from either side.

> **A blank square means undecided.** Never fill one in because it feels right. Fill it in because a clue, or the rest of its row, leaves no other choice.

# Lesson 2

Now each neighbor has two things to match. Rosa, Felix and Mabel each made one jam (plum, fig or quince) and each used one container (a crock, a mason jar or a tin).

[[clues L2]]

## The staircase grid

With three categories, the grid has three boxes arranged like a staircase. The top row of boxes matches names with jams and with jars. The bottom box matches jars with jams. Every pair of categories meets exactly once.

[[grid L2 | caption: Three boxes: names and jams, names and jars, jars and jams.]]

## Clues 1 and 2

Clue 1 is a dot for Rosa and fig, in the names-and-jams box. Clue 2 is a dot for fig and tin, in the bottom box. Each dot brings its X's.

## Carrying a fact

Here is the big idea of this lesson. Rosa made the fig jam, and the fig jam was in the tin. So Rosa used the tin. Put a dot for Rosa and tin, in the names-and-jars box.

> **Carry the dot.** If A goes with B, and B goes with C, then A goes with C. The same is true for X's: if A goes with B, and B does not go with C, then A does not go with C.

[[grid L2 | O: Rosa=fig, fig=tin | new: Rosa=fig, fig=tin | caption: Clues 1 and 2. || O: Rosa=fig, fig=tin, Rosa=tin | new: Rosa=tin | caption: Carry it: Rosa used the tin.]]

## Finishing up

Clue 3 says Felix did not use the crock. The tin is Rosa's, so Felix used the mason jar, and Mabel used the crock.

Clue 4 says the quince jam was not in the mason jar. In the bottom box the tin already belongs to fig, so the quince jam was in the crock and the plum jam was in the mason jar.

Now carry again. Mabel used the crock, and the crock held the quince jam, so Mabel made the quince jam. Felix used the mason jar, which held the plum jam.

[[grid L2 | O: Rosa=fig, fig=tin, Rosa=tin, Felix=mason jar, Mabel=crock, quince=crock, plum=mason jar, Mabel=quince, Felix=plum | caption: The finished grid. Every box agrees with every other box.]]

> **Check every box.** When you finish, each of the three boxes should have exactly one dot in every row and column, and the boxes should tell the same story.

# Lesson 3

Four neighbors waited in line at the bakery counter one morning. Who stood where?

[[clues L3]]

A line-up is a grid like any other, with places in line instead of pies. But order clues let you rule out more than you might think.

## "Somewhere ahead" is not "right ahead"

Clue 1 says Priya stood somewhere ahead of Jonah. It does **not** say she was right in front of him. Someone could stand between them.

What clue 1 does tell you is this: Priya cannot be last, because someone stands behind her. And Jonah cannot be first, because someone stands ahead of him.

> **Before does not mean immediately before.** "Ahead of," "earlier than" and "before" leave room for others in between. Only words like "right ahead of," "right behind" or "next to" mean no gap.

## "Right behind" means no gap

Clue 2 says Teddy stood right behind Priya. So Teddy cannot be first, and Priya cannot be last.

Clues 3 and 4 are plain X's: Lena was not first, and Jonah was not fourth.

[[grid L3 | X: Lena=first, Jonah=fourth, Priya=fourth, Jonah=first, Teddy=first | caption: Every X the clues give on a first reading.]]

## Who is first?

Look down the column for first place. Lena, Jonah and Teddy are crossed out. So Priya was first. Teddy is right behind her, so Teddy was second. Jonah was not fourth, so Jonah was third, and Lena was fourth.

[[grid L3 | O: Priya=first, Teddy=second, Jonah=third, Lena=fourth | new: Priya=first, Teddy=second, Jonah=third, Lena=fourth | caption: The line, front to back: Priya, Teddy, Jonah, Lena.]]

> **Read down the columns too.** In line-ups, the question "Who could be first?" is often quicker than "Where could Jonah be?"

## Other order words

- "Lena stood **exactly two places** behind Teddy": count. If Teddy is first, Lena is third.
- "**Next to**": right in front or right behind, either side.
- "**At one end** of the line": first or last.

# Lesson 4

At the county fair, the last caramel apple vanished from the candy stand. Three people were nearby, and each said one thing.

[[liar L4]]

## One suspect at a time

Here is the method. Pretend one person did it. Then go through the statements one by one and decide whether each would be true or false. Count the true ones. If the count matches the rule, that suspect fits. Then try the next suspect.

Exactly one suspect will fit.

## Testing Bea

Suppose Bea ate the caramel apple. Bea's own statement, "Kit didn't eat the last caramel apple," would be true. Hugo's, "Kit ate the last caramel apple," would be false. Kit's, "Either Bea or Hugo ate the last caramel apple," would be true. That is two true statements, but the rule says exactly one. So it was not Bea.

Test Hugo and Kit the same way. Only Kit gives exactly one true statement.

[[liar L4 filled]]

> **The shortcut: opposites.** When two people say opposite things, as Bea and Hugo do here, exactly one of them is telling the truth. Here that uses up the only true statement, so Kit's statement must be false. Neither Bea nor Hugo did it, so it was Kit.

## Things to watch for

- "I didn't do it" is true for everyone except the culprit.
- "Hugo is lying" is true exactly when Hugo's statement is false.
- Some puzzles use a different rule: "only the culprit is fibbing," which means the culprit's statement is false and everyone else's is true. The method is the same.

# Lesson 5

Three old friends keep boats in the harbor. They are 60, 65 and 70 years old, and they own a dory, a skiff and a sloop.

[[clues L5]]

## Comparisons rule out the ends

Clue 1 says Walt is older than Mabel. So Walt cannot be the youngest, 60, and Mabel cannot be the oldest, 70. Mark those two X's straight away.

## A pair clue

Clue 2 says: of Mabel and the skiff's owner, one is 65 and the other is 70. A pair clue tells you three things at once.

- Mabel is 65 or 70.
- The skiff's owner is 65 or 70.
- Mabel is not the skiff's owner, because they are two different people.

[[grid L5 | X: Walt=60, Mabel=70, Mabel=60, skiff=60, Mabel=skiff | caption: Clues 1 and 2, each read on its own.]]

## Two clues together

Now let them team up. Clue 2 says Mabel is 65 or 70. Clue 1 says she is not 70. So Mabel is 65, and by clue 2 the skiff's owner is 70.

Clue 1 again: Walt is older than Mabel, who is 65, so Walt is 70. That makes Walt the skiff's owner, and Nico is 60.

Finally, clue 3: Nico does not own the dory, and the skiff is Walt's, so Nico owns the sloop and Mabel owns the dory.

[[grid L5 | O: Mabel=65, Walt=70, Nico=60, Walt=skiff, Nico=sloop, Mabel=dory, skiff=70, sloop=60, dory=65 | caption: The finished grid.]]

> **Go back to the clues.** A clue that told you only a little at first can tell you much more once other facts are in. When you are stuck, read every clue again, slowly.

## Number words

- "**Older than**," "**cost more than**," "**later than**": the first person cannot have the smallest number, and the second cannot have the largest.
- "**Exactly 10 years older**": the gap is fixed. If the younger is 60, the older is 70.
- "**One hour after**": a gap of exactly one step.

# Lesson 6

Sometimes every clue has told you all it can, and the grid still has blanks. That is when you use a "Suppose." It is not guessing. It is testing.

Four neighbors returned library books one morning, one each hour from 9 a.m. to noon.

[[clues L6]]

## As far as the clues go

Clue 4 says Dot came earlier than the atlas borrower, so Dot did not borrow the atlas. By clue 2, Dot borrowed the cookbook and Wren the atlas. Clue 5 leaves Basil only the almanac, so Otis borrowed the diary.

The time clues give a few X's. Clue 1: the diary was not at 9 a.m., and the almanac was not at noon. Clue 3: the cookbook was not at noon. Clue 4: Dot was not at noon, and the atlas was not at 9 a.m. Carry each X across to the names.

[[grid L6 | O: Dot=cookbook, Wren=atlas, Basil=almanac, Otis=diary | X: diary=9 a.m., almanac=noon, cookbook=noon, Dot=noon, atlas=9 a.m., Otis=9 a.m., Basil=noon, Wren=9 a.m. | caption: Names and books are done. The times are not.]]

That is all the clues can say. The times still have several choices. So we test one.

## Suppose

Pick a row with only two or three choices left and test one of them in pencil. Suppose Dot came at 9 a.m.

Then the cookbook came at 9 a.m. By clue 3, the diary came one hour later, at 10 a.m. By clue 1, the almanac came earlier than the diary, so at 9 a.m. But 9 a.m. already belongs to Dot and her cookbook. Two people cannot come at the same time. The supposition breaks.

[[grid L6 | O: Dot=cookbook, Wren=atlas, Basil=almanac, Otis=diary, Dot=9 a.m., cookbook=9 a.m., diary=10 a.m. | X: almanac=11 a.m., almanac=noon, Basil=noon | new: Dot=9 a.m., cookbook=9 a.m., diary=10 a.m. | caption: In pencil: if Dot came at 9 a.m., the almanac has nowhere to go.]]

So Dot did not come at 9 a.m. Erase the pencil marks and put a firm X there.

Now the clues work again. Who came at 9 a.m.? Not Dot. Not Wren, whose atlas came later than Dot (clue 4). Not Otis, whose diary came later than the almanac (clue 1). So Basil and the almanac came at 9 a.m. Who came at 10 a.m.? Not Otis: his diary came one hour after Dot's cookbook (clue 3), and Dot came at 10 a.m. at the earliest. Not Wren: her atlas came later than Dot (clue 4). So Dot came at 10 a.m., the diary at 11 a.m., and Wren's atlas at noon.

> **Suppose in pencil, conclude in pen.** Write "Suppose" above the grid. If you reach a contradiction, erase everything you added and mark one firm X on the square you tested. If you do not reach a contradiction, the test tells you nothing: erase it and try another square.

## When to Suppose

- Only after every clue has been read again and nothing new comes.
- Choose a row with two choices if you can. Then a contradiction settles the row at once.
- One Suppose at a time. Every five-star puzzle in this book needs only one.
